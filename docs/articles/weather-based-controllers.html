Weather-Based Controllers: A Local Irrigation Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Weather-based controllers in Clearwater

Weather-based controllers, known as ET controllers or smart irrigation controllers, are automated devices that modify watering schedules according to current local weather information such as temperature, humidity, wind speed, solar radiation and rainfall. These units replace the rigid timer logic found in standard irrigation controllers with logic that matches actual landscape requirements. Weather-based controllers belong to the category of irrigation controllers and form part of the larger group of automated sprinkler management equipment. Parts and scope of a weather-based controller

Weather-based controllers include a controller unit, a control panel with wiring terminals, communication modules and at least one rain sensor. These elements combine to convert weather data into zone-specific run-time changes. The controller unit acts as the central processor. It collects data, performs an evapotranspiration (ET) calculation and generates a revised schedule with no manual entry required. Rain sensors connect to the system and track precipitation in millimeters per hour, halting a scheduled cycle as soon as rainfall exceeds the set sensitivity threshold. Communication modules link the controller to either a local weather station or a cloud-based weather network. Wiring terminals connect each irrigation zone to the control panel so that adjustments reach every sprinkler head. A lawn sprinkler system contractor assembles these components at installation and confirms that every terminal maps correctly to its zone.

Measurable attributes of weather-based controllers

Weather-based controllers have several measurable attributes that affect suitability for a specific property. The number of zones supported usually falls between 4 and 16 zones, which covers everything from a small residential lawn to a mid-size commercial landscape. Rain sensor sensitivity can be adjusted and is rated in millimeters per hour, which allows a technician to select the precise precipitation rate that suspends irrigation. Wireless communication module range differs by model and site conditions. The power source is 24V AC, the same standard used across most residential and light-commercial irrigation systems. Programming capability appears in the level of schedule detail. The controller can be configured to react to temperature changes of a set number of degrees before it recalculates run times. Water savings from a properly calibrated weather-based controller installation reach measurable percentages compared to a fixed-schedule timer because the system skips watering a zone when recent rainfall or an incoming storm already meets the water requirement. A smart irrigation controller shares most of these attributes and frequently serves as another name for the ET controller category.

How weather-based controllers relate to the broader irrigation system

Weather-based controllers function as one part of a complete sprinkler system that sprinkler repair professionals service across all system sizes. The controller is positioned upstream of every zone valve. Its signals open and close the valves, which means an incorrect controller setting influences all zones at once. Sprinkler repair work frequently starts at the controller when a homeowner states that zones activate at incorrect times or fail to run, since the controller is the central point that directs the entire system. Weather-based controllers vs. Time-based and manual controllers

A time-based controller operates every zone according to a fixed schedule without regard for rainfall, temperature or soil moisture. It waters on Tuesday and Saturday whether the lawn requires water or not. A manual sprinkler timer demands that a person physically update the schedule whenever conditions change, an action that seldom occurs. Manual controllers provide zero automation. They rely completely on the operator's attention. Weather-based controllers substitute that fixed logic with live data, so the schedule updates automatically when a cold front lowers temperatures or a rain event saturates the soil. The outcome is that a property using a weather sensor controller prevents both overwatering in wet periods and underwatering in heat events. These are two failure modes that a basic irrigation controller cannot correct by itself.

Common problems and signs a weather-based controller needs service

Weather-based controllers display distinct failure signs that set them apart from zone-valve or sprinkler-head issues. Zones that activate during or right after rain show that the rain sensor has lost its link to the controller unit or that the sensitivity threshold sits too high for local rainfall intensity. Some zones never activate. When this happens in dry conditions, the cause is often a communication module that no longer receives weather data, which leaves the controller without the ET input required to compute run times. Wiring terminal corrosion leads to random-seeming zone failures that originate from one loose or oxidized connection at the control panel. A controller that displays the correct schedule yet does not open valves on schedule normally indicates a 24V AC power supply problem instead of a programming error. Sprinkler inspection determines the active failure mode before any components are replaced. This approach reduces time spent on properties with 8 or more zones, where an incorrect diagnosis requires checking every terminal one by one.

Where weather-based controllers apply in Clearwater

Clearwater's climate matches the strengths of weather-based controllers. Summer afternoon thunderstorms can drop an inch of rain in under an hour and then give way to two weeks of dry heat. A fixed-schedule timer cannot adjust for that pattern, so properties with time-based controllers commonly overwater in June and underwater in October. ET controllers monitor local conditions without pause and revise zone run times to match actual evapotranspiration rates, which in Clearwater vary markedly between the wet season and the dry season. Weather-based controllers can be added to new construction or installed as a retrofit on an existing system. The wiring terminals and 24V AC power source work with most sprinkler systems already in place, so a retrofit normally requires no new pipe work. Only a controller exchange and sensor placement are needed.
