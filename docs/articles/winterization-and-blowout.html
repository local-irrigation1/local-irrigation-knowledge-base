Winterization and Blowout: A Local Irrigation Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Winterization and blowout in Clearwater

Winterization and blowout, known as sprinkler blowout or irrigation winterization, drains and purges water from a sprinkler system’s pipes, valves, heads, and backflow device with compressed air. The process leaves no standing water inside the system. Removal of every drop prevents water from freezing, expanding, and cracking components when temperatures fall. Winterization and blowout counts as sprinkler system maintenance completed at the close of the irrigation season, before ground temperatures reach levels that endanger the lines. Scope and component parts of a winterization and blowout service

A complete winterization and blowout service addresses every water-bearing section of a sprinkler system, including the main supply line. The water shutoff closes first to isolate the entire system. A technician then attaches a compressed-air source to the blowout valve and sends air through each zone one at a time, pushing water out the sprinkler heads until the flow runs completely dry. A lawn sprinkler system contractor performs the work zone by zone to keep pressure within safe limits and avoid overloading any pipe segment.

The component parts addressed in a full winterization and blowout service are listed below.

  • Water shutoff: Water shutoff stops supply flow before any air is introduced, protecting the compressor and preventing backflow.
  • Blowout valve: Blowout valve is the connection point where the air compressor attaches to the system's mainline.
  • Sprinkler pipes: Sprinkler pipes carry the compressed air zone by zone until standing water is expelled through the heads.
  • Zone-by-zone air purge: Zone-by-zone air purge cycles through each irrigation zone individually, typically for 2 to 3 minutes per zone, until no water discharges.
  • Backflow device draining: Backflow device draining removes water trapped in the backflow preventer, which is one of the most freeze-vulnerable components on the system.
  • System drain: System drain handles any residual water in low points that compressed air alone may not fully clear.

Measurable attributes of sprinkler blowout

Sprinkler blowout stays inside defined pressure and timing limits that safeguard the system against harm. Air pressure stays between 80 and 120 PSI on most residential systems. The lower figure suits flexible poly pipe while the higher figure fits rigid PVC. Pressure above 120 PSI can dislodge head seals and crack fittings, therefore readings stay under constant watch. Each zone usually clears in 2 to 3 minutes. A system containing 6 to 8 zones finishes the full procedure in 20 to 30 minutes from initial connection through final check.

Water removal speed during blowout varies with pipe diameter and the compressor’s cubic-feet-per-minute rating. CFM sets the clearing pace. A compressor rated at 20 to 25 CFM empties a standard residential zone in one pass. Finished irrigation winterization keeps the system safe through the cold months until spring startup returns water to the lines, since no liquid water stays inside to expand. How winterization and blowout relates to the broader sprinkler system

Winterization and blowout forms one category of sprinkler system maintenance located at the seasonal change from active irrigation to dormancy. It links directly to seasonal sprinkler adjustment, which modifies run times and zone coverage as weather shifts across the year. The blowout serves as the last adjustment before the controller switches to off or rain-delay mode for the winter.

The direct counterpart to winterization and blowout is spring startup, known as system reactivation or sprinkler start-up. Spring startup follows the opposite order: the water shutoff opens, every zone receives inspection for broken heads or altered coverage, and run times receive new programming for the active growing season. Each service stands independent. A system that omits winterization and blowout and then undergoes spring startup after a hard freeze commonly reveals cracked pipes, damaged head bodies, and a nonfunctional backflow preventer on first pressurization. Irrigation system repair corrects many of those resulting issues when they appear.

Common problems that make winterization and blowout necessary

Winterization and blowout prevents a defined group of freeze-damage failures that prove expensive to fix. The problems below rank among the most frequent results of skipping the service.

  • Cracked pipes: Cracked pipes result when standing water freezes and expands inside the line, splitting PVC or poly at joints and mid-run.
  • Blown sprinkler heads: Blown sprinkler heads occur when water trapped in the body freezes and forces the cap or nozzle off the riser.
  • Backflow preventer failure: Backflow preventer failure is one of the costliest freeze outcomes because the device sits above ground and is fully exposed to ambient temperature.
  • Valve damage: Valve damage happens when water in the valve body freezes and cracks the diaphragm or solenoid housing, causing the zone to run continuously or not at all after thaw.
  • Controller errors: Controller errors after a freeze often trace back to wiring shorts caused by water intrusion at damaged valve boxes rather than the controller itself.

Where winterization and blowout applies

Winterization and blowout applies to any in-ground sprinkler system that carries pressurized water through buried or exposed pipes in a climate where temperatures drop to or below 32°F. In Clearwater and coastal Pinellas County, hard freezes occur infrequently yet still happen, and one overnight freeze suffices to crack an unprepared system. Irrigation winterization holds equal importance for residential lawn systems, commercial landscape irrigation, and drip systems serving planting beds, since all retain standing water in the lines once the controller shuts down.

Scheduling winterization and blowout before the first forecast freeze, rather than after, remains standard industry practice. Timing counts. A system already frozen cannot undergo safe blowout until it thaws, and the damage may already exist.
