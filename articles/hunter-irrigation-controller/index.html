Component parts of a Hunter sprinkler timer

A Hunter Irrigation Controller is built from six core components that work together to translate a programmed schedule into timed electrical signals at each zone valve. The circuit board is the processing core, reading the schedule and triggering outputs in sequence. The transformer steps 120V AC household current down to 24 VAC, the operating voltage that opens and closes solenoid valves in the field. How a Hunter Irrigation Controller fits into a sprinkler system

A Hunter Irrigation Controller sits in the sprinkler system's control hierarchy upstream of every valve, head and pipe in the ground. The controller sends a 24 VAC signal down the zone wire to the solenoid on each valve; the solenoid opens the valve, water flows to the heads in that zone, and the controller closes the circuit when the programmed runtime ends. A rain sensor or soil moisture sensor connects to the sensor terminals on the controller and can interrupt a scheduled cycle when the ground is already wet, cutting water use without reprogramming. Weather-based controllers extend this further by pulling evapotranspiration data and adjusting runtimes automatically. Because the controller governs every zone, a wiring fault, a blown fuse or a corrupted program affects the entire system, not one head alone, which is why Hunter Irrigation Controller troubleshooting is the starting point for most multi-zone failures. Hunter Irrigation Controller versus manual valves and hose bib timers

Hunter Irrigation Controller is not a manual valve, a manual sprinkler valve or a hose bib timer. A manual valve requires a person to turn it on and off by hand at the valve box, with no scheduling, no zone sequencing and no sensor input. A hose bib timer attaches to an outdoor spigot and runs a single outlet on a basic mechanical or single-program digital cycle, typically covering one hose or one drip line. The Hunter sprinkler timer manages 4 to 48 independent zones simultaneously, stores multiple programs, accepts sensor overrides and, on Wi-Fi-enabled models, receives remote commands. The practical difference is that a manual valve or hose bib timer cannot scale to a full lawn irrigation system without a person present for every watering event. Programming Hunter controller: schedules, programs and run order

Programming a Hunter controller follows a consistent sequence regardless of model generation. A technician sets the current time and date first. Next, a program letter is selected, A, B, C, or D depending on the model, followed by the days that program runs, the start time, and the runtime for each active zone in sequence. Zones not used in that program are set to zero minutes and skipped automatically. Most Hunter models allow multiple start times per program, so a single program can run twice in one day without creating a second program. Seasonal adjust, available on most models, scales all runtimes up or down by a percentage without changing individual zone settings. Useful for transitioning from summer peak demand into cooler months. Current Hunter models retain programming in non-volatile memory. A brief power outage does not erase the schedule. Common Hunter Irrigation Controller problems in Clearwater

Hunter Irrigation Controller troubleshooting in Clearwater typically starts with four failure patterns that account for most service calls. A zone that does not run usually points to a wiring fault at the terminal strip, a failed solenoid on the valve, or a blown output fuse on the circuit board. A controller that runs every zone at once indicates a short on the common wire, which the board reads as a continuous ground across all outputs. A display that goes blank or shows corrupted characters after a power surge points to transformer or circuit board damage, since the 24 VAC supply runs through the transformer continuously. Scheduling drift, where the controller runs at the wrong time of day, is almost always a dead or weak backup battery that fails to hold the clock during a power interruption. Clearwater's high humidity and frequent afternoon storms accelerate corrosion at the terminal strip, particularly on outdoor enclosures that are not rated for the local rainfall intensity, making periodic terminal inspection a standard part of sprinkler maintenance in this region. System calibration can restore accurate zone timing after such environmental wear occurs.
