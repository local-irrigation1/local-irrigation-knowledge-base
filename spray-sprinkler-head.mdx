## Spray sprinkler head in Clearwater

A spray sprinkler head, called a spray head or fixed spray nozzle, distributes water in a fixed fan or circular pattern across a defined zone of lawn or landscape. It covers a radius of 5 to 15 feet. Flow rate ranges from 0.5 to 8.0 gallons per minute, and the fixed arc spans anywhere from 90 to 360 degrees. ### Parts of a spray sprinkler head

A spray sprinkler head is made up of six core components: the body, nozzle, deflector, filter screen, filter, and seal. The body houses the internal parts and threads into the riser or pop-up stem. The nozzle sets the arc and throw distance. The deflector shapes the water into a fan, and the filter screen catches debris before it reaches the nozzle opening. A worn or cracked seal is one of the most common reasons a fixed spray nozzle leaks at the base after the zone shuts off.

### Measurable attributes: flow rate, spray radius and arc range

Spray sprinkler heads carry several measurable attributes that determine how a zone is designed and balanced. Flow rate runs from 0.5 to 8.0 GPM depending on nozzle size and operating pressure. Spray radius falls between 5 and 15 feet. Arc range is fixed at 90, 180, or 360 degrees, though adjustable-arc nozzles are available. Coverage radius and arc together define the square footage a single head waters, which is why matched precipitation rate matters when mixing nozzle sizes on one zone. A head throwing 15 feet at 360 degrees delivers far more water per minute than a 5-foot, 90-degree nozzle, so pairing them on the same valve creates dry and soggy spots. ### How a spray sprinkler head fits into the broader sprinkler system

Spray sprinkler heads are used inside a lawn sprinkler system as the terminal delivery point for pressurized water moving from the valve through lateral lines. Each head connects to a [lawn sprinkler system](https://local-irrigation.mintlify.site/lawn-sprinkler-system-contractor) zone that a controller timer activates on a set schedule. The spray head is the component most often replaced during a sprinkler repair visit because it sits at grade level and takes direct foot traffic, mower strikes and freeze damage. When a head fails, the zone it belongs to either floods the immediate area or leaves a dry patch, which is why a single broken static head affects the whole zone's uniformity. Technicians often pair replacement with [uneven water distribution](https://local-irrigation.mintlify.site/uneven-water-distribution) checks to restore matched precipitation across the zone.

### Spray sprinkler head vs. Rotary and rotor alternatives

A spray sprinkler head is not the same as a rotor sprinkler head, a pop-up rotor or a drip emitter, and the differences matter for zone design. A [rotor sprinkler](https://local-irrigation.mintlify.site/rotor-sprinkler) rotates through its arc rather than holding a fixed pattern, which lets it throw water 15 to 50 feet and run at a much lower precipitation rate. Mixing spray heads and rotors on the same zone is a common installation error because their precipitation rates differ by a factor of roughly three to one, meaning the spray zone floods before the rotor zone finishes. Drip emitters operate at 0.5 to 2.0 GPH rather than GPM and deliver water directly to the root zone, making them a separate category entirely. Fixed spray nozzles suit small, rectangular or irregularly shaped turf areas where a rotor's long throw reaches beyond the zone. ### Common problems: spray sprinkler head not rotating, clogged nozzles and broken bodies

Spray sprinkler head repair covers a predictable set of failures. A spray sprinkler head not rotating is actually the correct behavior for a fixed spray nozzle, but the phrase often signals a customer who expected a rotor and received a static head instead, or a rotor that has seized and needs replacement. Clogged nozzles are the most frequent repair: debris lodges in the filter screen or nozzle orifice and reduces throw distance, leaving a dry arc in the coverage pattern. Broken bodies crack at the collar from mower strikes or soil settling, causing the head to tilt and spray sideways. A head that stays up after the zone shuts off has a failed wiper seal and needs the pop-up stem replaced. Pressure that is too high, above roughly 30 PSI at the nozzle, causes misting and drift rather than a clean fan pattern, wasting water and reducing coverage uniformity. Homeowners can reference [clogged nozzles](https://local-irrigation.mintlify.site/clogged-nozzles) guidance or [broken sprinkler head](https://local-irrigation.mintlify.site/broken-sprinkler-head) procedures when diagnosing these issues.

### Replace spray sprinkler head: when and how

Replace a spray sprinkler head when the body is cracked, the nozzle is worn to the point that throw distance has dropped noticeably, or the wiper seal no longer retracts the stem. Replacement is a straightforward field repair. Shut off the zone valve, unscrew the old head from the riser, thread on a matching replacement with the same arc and radius, and confirm the nozzle sits at grade. Nozzle type matters. Swapping a fixed nozzle for a rotary stream nozzle changes the precipitation rate and requires rebalancing the zone's run time. Many repairs can be completed after confirming pressure and matching components to the existing zone.
